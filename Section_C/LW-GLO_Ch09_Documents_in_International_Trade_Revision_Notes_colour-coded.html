2. Core Knowledge

2.1 Bills of lading (C1(a)) [level 1]

Chapter 09 pp.60–61. Level [1]: define the document, name its functions and types, identify the right document or party in a one-line scenario. Not tested in the four current reports; examined as an essay in June 2011 (meaning of a bill of lading and its role in the passage of risk).

2.1.1 Definition [M]

  • Means: a bill of lading is a document issued by the carrier (or the master or the carrier's agent) to the shipper, acknowledging that the carrier has received the goods and that they have been loaded on board a named vessel bound for a named destination, and stating the terms on which the goods are carried (ACCA F4 GLO June 2011 model answer). [CORRECTED #11: the chapter says "issued ... to a seller (or agent)"; the recipient is the shipper, often but not necessarily the seller]
  • Convention definition: a document which evidences a contract of carriage by sea and the taking over or loading of the goods by the carrier, and by which the carrier undertakes to deliver the goods against surrender of the document (Hamburg Rules 1978 Art 1) [ADDED: Hamburg Rules Art 1].
  • Remember: three ideas inside the definition: receipt, contract terms, delivery against surrender.
  • Tested as: 1 mark, "Which party issues a bill of lading?" (the carrier); "To whom?" (the shipper).
  • Trap: the chapter's "seller" is wrong where the buyer booked the carriage: an FOB buyer is the shipper.
  • Applied (illustrative): Mela Ltd sells FOB and the buyer's forwarder books the ship. The carrier issues the bill to the forwarder as shipper, not to Mela Ltd.

2.1.3 Operation: from loading to delivery [M]

The sequence in which the bill moves and what each step achieves (chapter p.60, reorganised).

  1. Shipper hands goods to the carrier → carrier issues a bill (a "received for shipment" bill before loading; on demand a "shipped" bill after loading, against surrender of any earlier document of title — Hague-Visby Art III r.7 [ADDED]).
  2. Bill issued "to order" → the carrier holds the goods for whoever is the lawful holder; the seller as shipper is the first holder (chapter: "the carrier therefore holds the goods on behalf of the seller").
  3. Seller presents the bill with the other documents to a bank for payment under a documentary credit, or to a collecting bank against payment or acceptance of a bill of exchange (chapter: "the seller will present the bill to a bank to obtain payment").
  4. Seller endorses and delivers the bill → bank → buyer, each becoming holder in turn (chapter: "indorse the bill by signing it (negotiate it), thereby transferring title to the goods").
  5. Buyer, as lawful holder, presents an original at the port of discharge → carrier delivers against surrender (Hamburg Rules Art 1 [ADDED]).
  6. All rights of suit under the contract of carriage vest in the lawful holder as if a party (COGSA 1992 s.2(1) [ADDED; L, conditional]). No transfer where, when the person becomes holder, possession of the bill no longer gives a right against the carrier to possession of the goods (s.2(2)).
  • Remember: under a documentary credit the bill must show shipment on board a named vessel at the port of loading stated in the credit, by pre-printed wording or a dated on-board notation (UCP 600 Art 20(a)(ii) [ADDED]).
  • Tested as: true/false, "The carrier may deliver to a buyer who produces a photocopy of the bill" (false: an original must be surrendered). Section B: who may sue the carrier for transit damage (the lawful holder).
  • Applied (illustrative): Bank Z pays the seller under a credit and holds the endorsed bill; the buyer has not reimbursed Bank Z. The carrier must not release the cargo without an original; Bank Z controls delivery.

2.1.4 Link to the passing of risk [M — cross-reference]

  • Chapter p.60: the bill "evidences the timing of the passing of goods to the carrier; normally, therefore, also the passing of risk to the buyer ... unless the parties have separately agreed".
  • Trap: the bill does not itself pass risk. It evidences the event (handing over to the carrier, or loading) at which risk passes under the sale contract: CISG Art 67 where the contract involves carriage, or the Incoterm chosen. Those rules are B2(d) and B1(c) and are not repeated here.
  • Applied (illustrative): shipped bill dated 3 May; damage at sea on 9 May. The dated bill evidences that risk had passed before the damage under a contract passing risk on shipment.

2.2.1 The credit-transfer chain [M]

[ADDED: UNCITRAL Model Law on International Credit Transfers Art 2; chain description from general knowledge, glossary agrees]

Originator → payment order 1 → Originator's bank → payment order 2 → Intermediary (correspondent) bank, used where the end banks have no account relationship or the currency clears in a third country → payment order 3 → Beneficiary's bank → accepts for the beneficiary → credits the Beneficiary.

  • Remember: each hop is a separate payment order with its own sender and receiving bank; obligations run link by link, not from the originator to a bank it never dealt with.
  • A credit transfer is a "push" payment (the payer's instruction moves the funds); a debit transfer (direct debit, cheque) is a "pull". The Model Law covers credit transfers only [ADDED: general knowledge] [UNVERIFIED]. The payment-order definition excludes instructions payable at the request of the beneficiary [UNVERIFIED].
  • Trap: credit transfer is not a letter of credit: one moves money unconditionally, the other is a bank's conditional undertaking to pay against documents. Dec 2013: "a fairly common error".
  • Tested as: "Which of the following is a sender?" (the originator and every sending bank). "The bank in the middle of the chain is the ..." (intermediary bank).
  • Applied (illustrative): Vela BV (Rotterdam) instructs its Dutch bank to pay US$1m to Nusantara PT at Bank X (Jakarta) via a New York correspondent. Vela = originator and first sender; Dutch bank = receiving bank of order 1, sender of order 2, originator's bank; New York bank = intermediary bank; Bank X = beneficiary's bank; Nusantara = beneficiary.

2.3.1 Nature, sphere and structure [M]

  • Means: a model law is a template for national legislation and binds no one until a State enacts it; a convention binds the States that ratify it. Adopted by UNCITRAL 15 May 1992 (uncitral.un.org). Enactment record: SOURCE DOES NOT ESTABLISH THIS.
  • Sphere: credit transfers where any sending bank and its receiving bank are in different States; a footnote allows extension to domestic transfers; consumer protection not dealt with [UNVERIFIED].
  • Structure (verified): Ch I Arts 1–4 general provisions (sphere, definitions, conditional instructions, variation by agreement); Ch II Arts 5–12 obligations of the parties (sender; payment to receiving bank; acceptance or rejection; obligations of receiving bank and of beneficiary's bank; execution period; revocation); Ch III Arts 13–18 consequences of failed, erroneous or delayed transfers (assistance; refund; underpayment; overpayment; interest; exclusivity); Ch IV Art 19 completion.
  • Party autonomy: rights and obligations may be varied by agreement except as provided (Art 4) [UNVERIFIED]. No agreement can bind a sender under a procedure that is not commercially reasonable (Art 5(3), verified); the refund duty is excludable only where a prudent originator's bank would not otherwise have accepted the order because of a significant risk (Art 14(2)) [UNVERIFIED].

Decision tree: who bears an unauthorised payment order (Art 5(1)–(4))

  1. Issued by the sender or someone with authority to bind it? Yes → sender bound (5(1)). No → 2.
  2. Authentication procedure beyond mere signature comparison? No → sender not bound. Yes → 3.
  3. Commercially reasonable and complied with by the bank? No → not bound, and no agreement can say otherwise (5(3)). Yes → 4.
  4. Can the sender prove the order came from an outsider (not an employee, former employee or access-holder)? No → bound (5(2)). Yes → 5.
  5. Can the bank prove the outsider got access through the sender's fault? Yes → bound. No → not bound (5(4)).
  • Applied (illustrative): a dismissed bookkeeper keeps Kestrel Ltd's online-banking token and sends a US$400,000 order which the bank verifies under the agreed token procedure. Kestrel is bound: commercially reasonable procedure, bank complied, order from a former employee. Had an outside hacker breached the bank's own systems with no fault of Kestrel, Kestrel would not be bound. Dec 2016 tested which party the procedure binds; Dec 2013 part (b) on unauthorised payments was "answered less well".

  • An accepting receiving bank (other than the beneficiary's bank) must issue, within the Art 11 time, a payment order to the beneficiary's bank or an appropriate intermediary bank consistent with the order it received (Art 8); a misdirected order or one lacking data must be notified to the sender within the same time.
  • The beneficiary's bank that accepts must place the funds at the beneficiary's disposal or apply the credit as instructed (Art 10).
  • Execution period (Art 11): in principle the bank executes on the banking day the order is received; if not, on the banking day after; an order received after the bank's cut-off time may be treated as received the next banking day; a later date specified in the order governs; late execution must be for value as of the day of receipt.
  • Trap: execution (the bank's own onward order or credit) is not completion (Art 19).
  • Applied (illustrative): an order reaches a Frankfurt bank at 16:30 on Friday, after its 15:00 cut-off. Treated as received on Monday; execution on Monday or Tuesday is within the period; execution on Wednesday is late and interest runs.

2.3.6 Revocation: Art 12 [H]

  • A revocation order must be authenticated (verified from official extract).
  • Timing: a payment order may not be revoked unless the revocation order is received by the receiving bank at a time and in a manner sufficient to afford the bank a reasonable opportunity to act before the later of the actual time of execution and the beginning of the day on which the order ought to have been executed under Art 11; at the beneficiary's bank, before the later of the actual time of completion and the beginning of the day when the funds are to be placed at the beneficiary's disposal [UNVERIFIED].
  • The sender and receiving bank may agree that orders are irrevocable, or that revocation is effective only if received earlier [UNVERIFIED]. The death, insolvency, bankruptcy or incapacity of the sender or originator does not of itself revoke a payment order or terminate the sender's authority [UNVERIFIED]. A sender whose order is effectively revoked is not obliged to pay for it [UNVERIFIED].
  • Tested as: Section B, "Can the importer recall the payment on Wednesday if its bank executed on Tuesday?" (no: revocation must reach the bank in time to act before execution).
  • Applied (illustrative): Vela BV sends an authenticated revocation at 09:00 on Tuesday; its bank had executed at 17:00 on Monday. Too late; the order stands. Had the bank not yet executed and had Tuesday been the day it ought to execute, a revocation received early on Tuesday in time to act would be effective.

2.3.8 Completion: Art 19 [H]

  • A credit transfer is completed when the beneficiary's bank accepts a payment order for the benefit of the beneficiary; on completion the beneficiary's bank becomes indebted to the beneficiary to the extent of the order accepted (Art 19(1)). Verified via reproduction.

  • Trap: payment to the receiving bank (Art 6) ≠ execution by a receiving bank (Arts 8, 11) ≠ completion (Art 19) ≠ funds actually available to the beneficiary.
  • Applied (illustrative): Bank X accepts the order for Nusantara PT on Thursday but posts the credit on Friday. The transfer completed on Thursday; from then Bank X, not the Dutch bank or Vela, owes Nusantara the money.

2.3.9 Putting the Model Law together [H]

Life of an international credit transfer, with the article that governs each step

  1. Originator issues payment order to its bank (Art 2). Authenticated? Sender bound (Art 5(1)–(4)).
  2. Originator's bank accepts: by agreement on receipt, by act, or by silence (Art 7(2)). Sender now obliged to pay it, due at start of execution period (Art 5(6)); paid by debit or credit (Art 6).
  3. Originator's bank executes within the execution period by issuing its own order to an intermediary or the beneficiary's bank (Arts 8, 11). Same cycle repeats at each intermediary.
  4. Revocation possible only if received in time to act before execution (Art 12).
  5. Beneficiary's bank accepts (Art 9(1)) → transfer completed; bank indebted to beneficiary (Art 19); funds placed at disposal (Art 10).
  6. Failure → refund with interest up the chain (Art 14); delay → interest only (Art 17); no other remedy save intent or recklessness (Art 18).

Mnemonic A-P-E-R-C — Acceptance, Payment obligation, Execution, Revocation window, Completion.

2.4.1 What the Convention is, and its status [M]

  • The United Nations Convention on International Bills of Exchange and International Promissory Notes was adopted by the UN General Assembly on 9 December 1988 (Res 43/165) [ADDED: uncitral.un.org]. Purpose: to overcome the major disparities and uncertainties between national laws governing instruments used for international payments [ADDED: UNCITRAL explanatory note]. 2.4.2 Definitions: bill of exchange, promissory note and the parties [H]

2.4.3 When the Convention applies: the words and the internationality test (Arts 1–2) [H]

  • Gateway 1, the words (Art 1): the instrument must contain the heading and, in its text, the words "International bill of exchange (UNCITRAL Convention)" or "International promissory note (UNCITRAL Convention)" [CORRECTED #2]. Both places, exact words, including the bracket.
  • Gateway 2, internationality (Art 2): the instrument specifies at least two of the following five places and indicates that any two so specified are situated in different States: (1) the place where the bill is drawn; (2) the place indicated next to the signature of the drawer; (3) the place indicated next to the name of the drawee; (4) the place indicated next to the name of the payee; (5) the place of payment [CORRECTED #3: "the place where the drawer signed" is imprecise; it is the place stated next to the signature]. Further proviso: the place where the bill is drawn or the place of payment must be specified and be in a Contracting State [UNVERIFIED; glossary agrees].
  • Mnemonic D-D-D-P-P — Drawn, Drawer's signature, Drawee's name, Payee's name, Place of payment.
  • Trap: "international" is tested by places written on the instrument, not by the parties' nationalities or where they live; two places in different States suffice even if the other three are blank; an instrument carrying the words but with all places in one State is not governed; an instrument with international places but without the words is not governed.
  • Excluded: cheques (Art 1). Chapter and glossary silent on the reason: SOURCE DOES NOT ESTABLISH THIS.
  • Tested as: 2 marks, "which TWO of the following make a bill international?" or true/false on whether the words are needed.
  • Applied (illustrative): a bill headed with the Convention words shows "Drawn at Lagos" beside the drawer's signature and "Payable at Bank K, Rotterdam" as the place of payment; no other places appear. Two places in different States: it is an international bill. If instead every place stated is in Nigeria, it is not, whatever the drawee's nationality.

2.4.4 A definite sum: Art 7 and the interest rules [H]

The rule tested in 2025–26 Q2 and entirely absent from the chapter [ADDED: Convention Art 7, verified].

The sum payable by an instrument is deemed to be a definite sum although the instrument states that it is to be paid: (a) with interest; (b) by instalments at successive dates; (c) by instalments at successive dates with a stipulation that upon default in payment of any instalment the unpaid balance becomes due; (d) according to a rate of exchange indicated in the instrument or to be determined as directed by the instrument; or (e) in a currency other than the currency in which the sum is expressed in the instrument.

Mnemonic I-I-A-X-C — Interest, Instalments, Acceleration clause, eXchange rate, Currency.

  • Interest: a stipulation that the sum is to be paid with interest is deemed not to have been written unless it indicates the rate at which interest is to be paid [ADDED: Convention, verified; article number (Art 8) UNVERIFIED]. The rate may be fixed or variable; a variable rate must vary in accordance with provisions stipulated in the instrument and by reference to one or more reference rates published or otherwise publicly available, may not be subject to unilateral determination by a person named in the instrument (unless named only in the reference-rate provisions), and the instrument may set limits to the permissible variation [ADDED: UNCITRAL explanatory note, verified].
  • Trap: the 2025–26 distractors: "bills payable 'with interest' are only definite if the interest is at a fixed rate" (false: variable reference rates are allowed); "bills must be for single payments rather than instalments" (false: (b) and (c)). Correct: exchange-rate bills (d) and other-currency bills (e).
  • Tested as: "which TWO statements are correct about a definite sum" (2 marks); 1 mark, "A bill payable in instalments is not for a definite sum" (false).
  • Applied (illustrative): a Convention bill for "EUR 100,000 payable in USD at the ECB reference rate on the due date, with interest at EURIBOR plus 2%". It is for a definite sum: (d), (e) and a variable reference-rate interest clause are all permitted. If it said "with interest" and no rate, the interest words would be treated as not written and the bill would be for EUR 100,000 without interest.

2.4.5 Time of payment: on demand or at a definite time (Art 9) [H]

The second half of Art 3(1)(b), which the chapter renders in 1882 Act language (p.57) [CORRECTED #20].

  • Payable on demand: an instrument which states that it is payable on demand or at sight or on presentment, or one in which no time of payment is expressed [UNVERIFIED].
  • Payable at a definite time if it states that it is payable: (a) on a stated date, or at a fixed period after a stated date, or at a fixed period after the date of the instrument; (b) at a fixed period after sight; (c) by instalments at successive dates; or (d) by instalments at successive dates with the stipulation that upon default the unpaid balance becomes due [ADDED: Art 9; items (a) and (d) verified, (b) and (c) UNVERIFIED].
  • The time of payment of a bill payable at a fixed period after sight is determined by the date of acceptance or, if the bill is dishonoured by non-acceptance, by the date of protest or, if protest is dispensed with, by the date of dishonour [ADDED: Art 9, verified].
  • Terminology: a sight bill is payable on presentation; a time (usance) bill is payable at a fixed future date or a fixed period after sight, giving the buyer credit [ADDED: glossary cross-check; general knowledge].
  • Trap: "90 days after sight" is a definite time even though the calendar date is unknown when the bill is drawn; the clock starts at acceptance.
  • Applied (illustrative): a bill "payable 90 days after sight" is presented for acceptance and accepted on 1 October 2026. Maturity is 30 December 2026. Had the drawee refused acceptance and protest been made on 3 October, the 90 days run from 3 October.

2.4.6 Transfer, endorsement and the holder (Arts 13–16) [H]

The chapter's endorsement chain (p.58) in Convention terms, including the point tested in Dec 2017.

  • An instrument is transferred (a) by endorsement and delivery of the instrument by the endorser to the endorsee, or (b) by mere delivery of the instrument if the last endorsement is in blank [ADDED: Convention; substance verified by examiner's report Dec 2017; article number (Art 13) UNVERIFIED] [CORRECTED #4: the chapter's "bearer bill" is this rule under the wrong name].
  • An endorsement must be written on the instrument or on a slip affixed to it (an "allonge"), signed; it must be unconditional; a conditional endorsement transfers the instrument whether or not the condition is fulfilled [ADDED: Convention; unconditional requirement verified via explanatory note; the rest UNVERIFIED].
  • Blank endorsement: a signature alone, or a signature with a statement that the instrument is payable to a person in possession of it (verified). Special endorsement: signature with a statement naming the person to whom the instrument is payable (chapter: "in favour of a specified person (the indorsee)"). A holder of a blank-endorsed instrument may convert the blank endorsement into a special one by writing in a name [UNVERIFIED].
  • Holder (Art 15, verified): (a) the payee in possession of the instrument; or (b) a person in possession of an instrument which has been endorsed to him, or on which the last endorsement is in blank, and on which there appears an uninterrupted series of endorsements, even if any endorsement was forged or was signed by an agent without authority.
  • Trap: possession is not enough for an instrument specially endorsed to someone else; the chain of endorsements must reach the possessor (or end in blank). A forged endorsement does not break the chain for holder status under Art 15, though the forgery still has consequences for liability (2.4.8) and for protected-holder status.
  • The chapter's chain in Convention language: payee endorses specially → endorsee endorses specially or in blank → ... → once an endorsement is in blank, any possessor may transfer by mere delivery and is a holder; the possessor may write in a name and continue the chain by endorsement.
  • Tested as: 1 mark, "In relation to the transfer of a bill endorsed in blank, which is true?" (it can be transferred by delivery) Dec 2017. True/false: "An endorsement may be made subject to a condition" (it may be written, but the condition is disregarded for transfer).
  • Applied (illustrative): Orion GmbH, payee, signs the back of the bill and hands it to Bank P without naming Bank P. The endorsement is in blank; Bank P is a holder and may transfer the bill to Bank Q by simply delivering it. Bank Q is also a holder. Neither Bank P nor Bank Q has signed, so neither is a party (2.4.8).

2.4.7 Protected holder and defences (Arts 28–32) [H]

The Convention's equivalent of the holder in due course; not in the chapter [ADDED: Convention Arts 28–32].

  • Protected holder (Art 29, core verified): the holder of an instrument which was complete when he took it, or which was incomplete within Art 12(1) and was completed in accordance with authority given, provided that when he became a holder he was without knowledge of the defences, claims and dishonour specified in the article, the time limit for presentment for payment had not expired, and he did not obtain the instrument by fraud or theft or participate in a fraud or theft concerning it [conditions beyond "complete" UNVERIFIED].
  • Every holder is presumed to be a protected holder unless the contrary is proved [ADDED: Convention, verified; article number (Art 32) UNVERIFIED].
  • Effect: a party may not set up against a protected holder the personal defences it could raise against an ordinary holder (for example defences based on the underlying transaction with a prior party); it may still raise defences such as a forged or unauthorised signature, material alteration, its own incapacity, and defences based on the underlying transaction between itself and that holder or the holder's own fraud [ADDED: Art 30] [UNVERIFIED detail]. An ordinary (non-protected) holder takes subject to defences based on the underlying transaction and to the other party's claims [ADDED: Art 28] [UNVERIFIED].
  • Trap: glossary cross-check: "for value" is a Bills of Exchange Act 1882 (holder in due course) requirement, not an Art 29 element; do not add it.
  • Tested as: 2 marks true/false: "Every holder is presumed to be a protected holder unless the contrary is proved" (true); "A protected holder is subject to the acceptor's defence that the goods were defective" (false).
  • Applied (illustrative): Bank Q takes a complete Convention bill before maturity, with no knowledge of any dispute, and presents it to the acceptor at maturity. The acceptor refuses to pay because the goods sold by the drawer were defective. That is a defence based on the underlying transaction with a prior party; it fails against Bank Q as protected holder.

2.4.10 Discharge and limitation (Arts 72–84) [M]

  • A party is discharged of liability when he pays the holder the amount due at or after maturity, against delivery of the instrument; payment by the acceptor or maker discharges all parties; payment by the drawer or an endorser discharges the parties after it and gives it rights against the parties before it [ADDED: Convention] [UNVERIFIED].
  • Limitation (Art 84, verified): a right of action arising on an instrument may no longer be exercised after four years have elapsed. Starting points [UNVERIFIED]: against the maker or the maker's guarantor of a note payable on demand, from the date of the note; against the acceptor, maker or their guarantors of an instrument payable at a definite time, from the date of maturity; against the drawer, endorsers and their guarantors, from the date of protest (or, where protest is dispensed with, from maturity); against a party who has paid and seeks to recover from prior parties, from the date of payment.
  • Trap: four years (Convention) v one year (Hague-Visby suit against the carrier).

2.4.11 Life of a Convention bill: the chapter's sequence corrected [H]

Chapter p.58, reorganised with the governing rule at each step.

  1. Drawer draws the bill on the drawee, payable to the payee or order, dated, signed, carrying the Convention words and two places in different States (Arts 1–3).
  2. Bill presented to the drawee for acceptance where required; drawee signs (with or without "accepted") and becomes the acceptor, primarily liable (Art 41). Refusal = dishonour by non-acceptance → protest and notice → recourse against the drawer.
  3. Accepted bill delivered to the payee; payee may hold to maturity or transfer it, typically by endorsement and delivery to a bank for immediate cash at a discount (chapter: "if they want the money now instead of at some fixed or determinable future time").
  4. Endorsements continue the chain (special or blank); after a blank endorsement, transfer by mere delivery is possible (Art 13); a good-faith taker is presumed a protected holder (Art 32).
  5. Holder presents for payment at maturity or within the two following business days.
  6. Paid → all parties discharged. Dishonoured by non-payment → protest within four business days, notice within two → recourse against acceptor, drawer, endorsers and guarantors (not against a transferor by mere delivery) within four years.

2.4.12 Promissory notes [H]

The half of the outcome the chapter omits entirely [ADDED: Convention].

  • A note is a promise by the maker to pay, not an order to a third person: two parties at the outset (maker, payee), no drawee, no acceptance (Art 3(2), verified).
  • The maker is liable from signature as the acceptor of a bill would be, and cannot exclude or limit that liability (Art 39, verified in substance). Contrast the drawer of a bill, who can draw "without recourse".
  • Words required: heading and text "International promissory note (UNCITRAL Convention)" (Art 1, verified); internationality by two places in different States (Art 2, the drawee place being irrelevant).
  • The rules on transfer, holders, protected holders, guarantee, presentment for payment, protest, notice and limitation apply to notes as to bills [UNVERIFIED as to the drafting technique]; there is no presentment for acceptance. Where the note is guaranteed without naming the party, the guarantee is for the maker (verified).
  • Tested as: 1 mark, "Which party is primarily liable on an international promissory note?" (the maker). True/false: "A promissory note must be accepted before the maker is liable" (false).
  • Applied (illustrative): Solano SA signs a document headed "International promissory note (UNCITRAL Convention)": "We promise to pay Rialto Ltd or order USD 200,000 on 30 June 2027", dated, place of making Buenos Aires, place of payment London. Solano is the maker, primarily liable from signature; Rialto is payee and holder. Any clause "maker not liable" is ineffective.

2.5.1 What a documentary credit is [H]

  • Means (chapter p.62): a means whereby the seller obtains payment against documents from a bank while the buyer still enjoys a period of credit before it pays; the buyer's bank stands between the two.
  • Definition: "Credit means any arrangement, however named or described, that is irrevocable and thereby constitutes a definite undertaking of the issuing bank to honour a complying presentation" (UCP 600 Art 2) [ADDED: UCP 600 Art 2, verified].
  • Governing rules: the ICC Uniform Customs and Practice for Documentary Credits, UCP 600 (ICC Publication No. 600, 2007 revision), approved by the ICC Banking Commission on 25 October 2006 and in effect from 1 July 2007 [ADDED: iccwbo.org, verified]; they apply to a credit that states it is subject to them [Art 1, UNVERIFIED]. The chapter never names UCP 600; every rule in this section comes from it.
  • Corrected position: the chapter says credits "should be arranged before the contract is entered into". Sequence: the sale contract stipulates payment by documentary credit; the buyer (applicant) then instructs its bank to issue the credit; the credit then operates independently of the contract (Art 4). [CORRECTED #19 — Unresolved: the chapter's sentence is retained as practice advice, UNVERIFIED]
  • Where it sits among payment methods [ADDED: general knowledge; glossary cross-check] [UNVERIFIED]: from most to least risky for the exporter: open account → documentary collection using a bill of exchange (documents against payment or acceptance) → unconfirmed credit → confirmed credit → payment in advance. The buyer's risk runs the other way.
  • Trap: credit transfer ≠ letter of credit (Dec 2013): a credit transfer moves funds unconditionally through a chain of payment orders; a letter of credit is a bank's conditional undertaking to pay against documents. A letter of credit is not a negotiable instrument; the bill of exchange (draft) drawn under an acceptance credit is.
  • Tested as: 1 mark, "Under UCP 600 a credit is ..." (an irrevocable undertaking of the issuing bank to honour a complying presentation). Section B: recommend a payment method for a first sale to an unknown buyer in a high-risk country (confirmed irrevocable credit).

2.5.3 The mechanism: the chapter's eight steps corrected [H]

  1. Sale contract concluded, stipulating payment by documentary credit; B (applicant) asks BB (issuing bank) to issue a credit in favour of S (beneficiary) (chapter step 1) [CORRECTED #19 on sequence].
  2. BB issues the credit → BB is irrevocably bound to honour a complying presentation from the time of issue (Arts 2, 7) (chapter step 2, "guaranteeing" corrected) [CORRECTED #13].
  3. BB asks SB (advising bank) to advise S that the credit has been issued; SB authenticates and transmits it, without any undertaking to honour or negotiate (Art 9(a)) (chapter step 3).
  4. SB agrees to handle its end. If BB has authorised or requested confirmation and SB agrees, SB adds its confirmation → a second, independent undertaking to honour or negotiate (Arts 2, 8) (chapter step 4: confirmed v unconfirmed).
  5. S ships the goods and presents the documents stipulated in the credit (typically transport document, invoice, insurance document) to SB within the credit's expiry date and presentation period [CORRECTED #14: not "proof of delivery / transfer of the goods"; banks deal with documents, not goods — Art 5] (chapter step 5).
  6. SB examines the documents on their face within a maximum of five banking days following the day of presentation (Art 14(b)). If SB has confirmed (or is a nominated bank willing to act) and the presentation complies, it honours or negotiates and forwards the documents to BB (Art 8). If SB is only an advising bank it forwards the documents and does not pay [CORRECTED #15] (chapter step 6).
  7. BB examines within its own five banking days and, if the presentation complies, honours: it pays or reimburses SB (Art 7). If discrepant, BB gives a single notice of refusal stating every discrepancy and the disposal of the documents, no later than the close of the fifth banking day; it may in its discretion approach B for a waiver [Art 16, UNVERIFIED] (chapter step 7).

2.5.7 Letters of comfort [H]

  • Means (chapter p.63): a letter, typically from a parent or holding company to a lender to (or major supplier of) its subsidiary, indicating the parent's intention to continue to support the subsidiary; used where the subsidiary is raising finance and the parent will not give a guarantee; "normally it is just a comfort", and "rarely does such a letter bind the parent", so if the subsidiary becomes insolvent the parent is not liable to settle its obligations.
  • Rule: whether a comfort letter creates a legal obligation depends on the construction of its words in their commercial context; a statement of the parent's present policy or intention is not a promise and is not enforceable; a clear promise as to future conduct can be contractually binding even though the document is called a "letter of comfort" [ADDED: BAILII-hosted judgments citing the case; the "present policy v future promise" formulation is from the glossary and UNVERIFIED against a law report].
  • Authority: Kleinwort Benson Ltd v Malaysia Mining Corporation Bhd [1989] 1 WLR 379 (CA): the comfort letter had no contractual effect; the court's task is to ascertain the parties' common intention from the language of the document and the surrounding circumstances (Ralph Gibson LJ). Facts as stated in the glossary [UNVERIFIED]: the parent refused a guarantee for its subsidiary's borrowing but wrote that "it is our policy to ensure that the business of [the subsidiary] is at all times in a position to meet its liabilities"; the subsidiary collapsed; the bank, having accepted comfort instead of a guarantee, had no claim.
  • Comparison [ADDED: general knowledge; glossary cross-check]: comfort letter — usually no legal obligation (moral or reputational pressure only); guarantee — a secondary obligation to pay if the principal debtor defaults; indemnity — a primary obligation to make good the loss whatever happens to the principal debt. Creditor protection runs weakest to strongest in that order. Formalities and the precise guarantee/indemnity rules are English contract law and are not examinable in GLO beyond this contrast.
  • Trap: the label does not decide the question; the words do. "We intend to maintain our shareholding" and "it is our policy that the subsidiary meets its liabilities" describe present intention; "we will ensure that the subsidiary pays" is a promise. A comfort letter is not a guarantee, and a lender who accepts one instead of a guarantee has priced the risk accordingly.
  • Tested as: 1 mark, "A letter of comfort is generally ..." (morally, not legally, binding on the parent). Section B: a parent's letter is quoted; state whether the lender can recover from the parent when the subsidiary defaults, and why.
  • Applied (illustrative): Kanto Holdings writes to Lender Bank: "We are aware of the US$10m facility to our subsidiary and it is our policy to ensure that it is in a position to meet its liabilities." The subsidiary defaults. Lender Bank has no claim against Kanto: the letter states present policy, not a promise to pay (Kleinwort Benson). Had Kanto written "we undertake to provide the subsidiary with the funds needed to repay the facility", the letter would be construed as a promise and Kanto could be liable in contract.

5. MCQ Traps & Discrimination Lines

Grouped by topic, then by question format. Each line ends with its priority tag.

5.1 Bills of lading

  1. A bill of lading is issued by the carrier (or master or agent) to the shipper; "to the seller" is wrong where the buyer booked the carriage. [M]
  2. Three functions: receipt, evidence of the contract of carriage, document of title; only a negotiable "to order" bill is a document of title. [H]
  3. A sea waybill and an air waybill are not documents of title, and an "airway bill" is not a bill of lading at all. [M]
  4. Straight bill = named consignee, not transferable; "to order" bill = negotiable by endorsement and delivery. "Made out in favour of the buyer" (chapter) describes the straight bill. [M]
  5. The bill evidences shipment; it does not itself pass risk — the sale contract (CISG Art 67, Incoterms) does. [M]
  6. Clean transport document: no clause or notation expressly declaring a defective condition of goods or packaging (UCP 600 Art 27); the word "clean" need not appear. [M]
  7. "Shipped" confirms loading; "received for shipment" does not; a credit requires on-board evidence (Art 20(a)(ii)). [M]
  8. The carrier delivers only against surrender of an original; a photocopy or a copy bill does not entitle the buyer to the goods. [M]
  9. Prima facie evidence of receipt as described becomes conclusive in favour of a good-faith third-party transferee or lawful holder (Hague-Visby Art III r.4; COGSA 1992 s.4). [L]
  10. One year is the Hague-Visby suit period against the carrier; four years is the Convention limitation on a bill or note. [L]

5.2 Bank transfers and the UNCITRAL Model Law

  1. Sender = the person who issues a payment order, including the originator and any sending bank; a sender need not be a bank; the originator is a sender (Dec 2013). [H]
  2. The sender pays the receiving bank when the receiving bank accepts; payment is due at the beginning of the execution period (Art 5(6)). Both halves of the correct option say "receiving bank" (2024–25 Q1). [H]
  3. "Beneficiary" belongs to credit transfers and letters of credit; "payee" belongs to bills and notes. [M]
  4. A credit transfer is not a letter of credit: unconditional movement of funds v conditional undertaking against documents (Dec 2013). [H]
  5. A payment order must be unconditional; "pay when the goods arrive" is not a payment order. [H]
  6. Silence is acceptance: a receiving bank that neither executes nor rejects within the time allowed has accepted (Arts 7(2)(e), 9(1)(h)). [H]
  7. Authentication: a purported sender is bound by an unauthorised order if the procedure was commercially reasonable and the bank complied; escape only by proving the order came from an outsider (not an employee, former employee or access-holder) and not through the sender's fault; the parties cannot agree to bind the sender under an unreasonable procedure (Art 5(2)–(4)). [H]
  8. Revocation must be authenticated and reach the bank in time to act before execution; once executed, too late; death, insolvency or incapacity of the sender does not itself revoke [UNVERIFIED] (Art 12). [H]
  9. An uncompleted transfer is refunded to the originator by the originator's own bank, with interest (Art 14); the originator does not chase the intermediary. [H]
  10. Liability for delay is interest only (Art 17); remedies are exclusive save specific intent to cause loss or recklessness with actual knowledge (Art 18); negligence is not the exception, and lost profits are not recoverable. [H]
  11. Completion = acceptance by the beneficiary's bank (Art 19); a deduction of charges does not stop completion; funds posted later does not delay it. [H]
  12. A model law is enacted by States; a convention is ratified. [M]
  13. Payment to the receiving bank (Art 6) ≠ execution (Arts 8, 11) ≠ completion (Art 19). [M]

5.3 The Bills and Notes Convention

  1. Convention bill: unconditional order, definite sum, to the payee or to his order, on demand or at a definite time, dated, signed by the drawer. "Or to bearer" and "sum certain" signal the 1882 Act (the chapter's wording), not the Convention. [H]
  2. The words "International bill of exchange (UNCITRAL Convention)" must appear in the heading and in the text; a heading alone, or the words without "(UNCITRAL Convention)", is insufficient. [H]
  3. International = at least two of five places stated, any two in different States; nationality or residence of the parties is irrelevant. [H]
  4. Cheques are excluded (Art 1), however international. [M]
  5. Status: not in force; ten ratifications then twelve months (Art 89(1)). [M]
  6. Definite sum survives interest, instalments, acceleration, exchange-rate and other-currency terms (Art 7); "only if the interest rate is fixed" and "single payments only" are false (2025–26 Q2). [H]
  7. "With interest" but no rate: the interest words are disregarded; the bill is still valid for the principal. [H]
  8. Acceptance = drawee's signature alone, or signature with "accepted" or similar words; "accepted" alone, a date alone, or an oral promise is not acceptance (2025–26 Q1). [H]
  9. Drawee: no liability until acceptance. Acceptor: primary. Maker of a note: primary from signature. Before acceptance the only person liable on the bill is the drawer (and any endorser). [H]
  10. The drawer may exclude liability "without recourse" if another party is or becomes liable (Art 38); the maker of a note cannot exclude (Art 39). [H]
  11. After a blank endorsement the instrument transfers by mere delivery (Dec 2017); "bearer bill" is not the Convention's term and bills cannot be drawn to bearer. [H]
  12. A transferor by mere delivery has not signed, is not a party and is not liable on the bill; it gives only the Art 45 representations to its transferee. [H]
  13. Every holder is presumed to be a protected holder unless the contrary is proved; "for value" is not a Convention requirement. [H]
  14. A protected holder takes free of personal defences (the acceptor's quarrel with the drawer about the goods) but not of real defences (forged signature, material alteration, incapacity) [UNVERIFIED detail]. [H]
  15. Protest within four business days; notice of dishonour within two business days. A missing protest discharges drawer and endorsers; a missing notice only makes the defaulter liable in damages [UNVERIFIED]. [H]
  16. Guarantee (aval): "guaranteed", "aval", "good as aval" or similar words with signature, or a signature alone on the front; unspecified guarantee covers the acceptor or drawee (bill) or the maker (note); "in case of need" is not the Convention's term. [M]
  17. Limitation: four years (Art 84). [H]
  18. "Indorse" is the 1882 Act spelling; the Convention says "endorse". [L]
  19. Promissory note: a promise by the maker; two parties; no drawee; no acceptance; an option saying a note must be accepted is wrong. [H]

5.4 Letters of credit

  1. Under UCP 600 a credit is irrevocable, even if it says nothing (Arts 2, 3); "revocable or irrevocable" is a pre-UCP 600 classification and a distractor. [H]
  2. Amendment or cancellation needs the agreement of the issuing bank, the confirming bank (if any) and the beneficiary (Art 10(a)); notifying the seller is not enough, and the applicant cannot amend. [H]
  3. Issuing bank must honour; confirming bank must honour or negotiate; an advising bank that has not confirmed has no undertaking (Arts 7, 8, 9(a)); the chapter's step 6 (advising bank pays) is only right for a confirming bank. [H]
  4. "Undertaking to honour", not "guarantee": the bank's obligation is primary and independent, not secondary to the buyer's default. [H]
  5. Autonomy: the credit is separate from the sale contract (Art 4) and banks deal with documents, not goods (Art 5); a bank cannot refuse because the goods are defective. [H]
  6. Five banking days following presentation to examine (Art 14(b)); banking days, not calendar days, not "a reasonable time"; refusal by one notice stating all discrepancies [Art 16 UNVERIFIED]. [H]
  7. Confirmed = two undertakings (issuing and confirming); unconfirmed = one (issuing only); count undertakings, not banks. [H]
  8. Four modes of availability: sight payment, deferred payment, acceptance, negotiation (Art 6(b)); only the acceptance credit involves a draft (bill of exchange) accepted by the bank. [M]
  9. A credit is transferable only if it states "transferable" [Art 38 UNVERIFIED]. [L]
  10. Sequence: sale contract → applicant instructs issuing bank → credit issued → advised (and possibly confirmed) → shipment → documents → examination → honour → reimbursement → documents to applicant; the chapter's "arrange the credit before the contract" is practice advice, not a rule. [M]

5.5 Letters of comfort

  1. A letter of comfort is normally a moral, not a legal, obligation; whether it binds depends on its words: a statement of present policy or intention does not; a clear promise as to future conduct can (Kleinwort Benson v Malaysia Mining Corporation Bhd [1989]). [H]
  2. Comfort letter (intention) ≠ guarantee (secondary promise to pay on default) ≠ indemnity (primary promise to make good). [M]

5.6 Question-format traps for this section

  1. "Which TWO": both selections must be right; look for the shared feature (in 2025–26 Q1 both correct options involved the drawee's signature). [H]
  2. NOT is capitalised; the question wants the one false statement, so three options will be true Convention or UCP rules. [M]
  3. Similar-word options: in Model Law questions underline who pays, to whom, and on whose acceptance (2024–25 Q1). [H]
  4. Too-narrow distractors: "only if fixed", "single payments only", "must be a bank", "must notify the seller" — an added restriction usually makes a true rule false. [H]
  5. Generic v specific: where each specific option fails for some case, the general option is the answer (examiner's 2023–24 Q4 method). [M]
  6. Numbers: 4 business days (protest), 2 business days (notice), 4 years (limitation), 10 ratifications + 12 months, 5 places, 5 banking days, and 1 year / 3 days / 666.67 / 2 belong to Hague-Visby, not the Convention. [H]
  7. Section B multi-task: identify the instrument first (bill, note, credit, transfer, comfort letter), then the party, then the rule, then apply; state the article or case only where you are sure of it. [H]
