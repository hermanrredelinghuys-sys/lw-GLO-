1.2 How Section C is examined

  • 1 mark, three options: one fact (which document is a document of title; who is primarily liable after acceptance; which words a Convention bill must carry). Report 2023–24: chosen to show that guessing "is extremely unlikely to be successful".
  • 2 marks, four options: near-identical wording. "pay the sending bank when the receiving bank accepts" v "pay the receiving bank when the receiving bank accepts" (report 2024–25 Q1: "similar words make it very difficult").
  • 2 marks, "which TWO": both needed; distractors state a rule too narrowly ("only if the interest rate is fixed"; "single payments only") (report 2025–26 Q1–Q2).
  • True/false pairs and negative stems (NOT is always capitalised).
  • Section B, 6 marks: short scenario, sub-questions: identify the instrument or party, state the rule, apply it. Every report: application is "not to the forefront of candidates' abilities".

1.3 Highest-priority areas

  1. Acceptance of a bill: signature alone, or signature with "accepted"; never the word alone or a date. 2025–26 Q1. Chapter gives only "signs as accepted" [CORRECTED #9].
  2. Definite sum: interest, instalments, acceleration, exchange rate or another currency do not stop a sum being definite. 2025–26 Q2. Absent from the chapter.
  3. The sender pays the receiving bank when the receiving bank accepts; payment is due at the start of the execution period. 2024–25 Q1. Absent.
  4. Sender includes the originator; a sender need not be a bank. Dec 2013. Absent.
  5. Authentication: who bears an unauthorised payment order. Dec 2016; Dec 2013. Absent.
  6. A bill endorsed in blank transfers by delivery. Dec 2017. Chapter calls it a "bearer bill" [CORRECTED #4].
  7. Credit transfer is not a letter of credit. Dec 2013.
  8. UCP 600 credits are irrevocable; amendment needs the beneficiary's agreement. Chapter teaches the opposite [CORRECTED #17, #18].
  9. Issuing bank must honour; confirming bank must honour or negotiate; an advising bank gives no undertaking. Chapter has the advising bank paying [CORRECTED #15].
  10. A comfort letter binds only if its words amount to a promise. Kleinwort Benson v Malaysia Mining Corporation Bhd [1989].

2. Core Knowledge

2.1 Bills of lading (C1(a)) [level 1]

Chapter pp.60–61. Define the document, name its functions and types, identify the right document or party.

2.1.1 Definition and issue [M]

  • Means: a document issued by the carrier to the shipper acknowledging receipt of the goods, their loading on board a named vessel bound for a named destination, and the terms on which they are carried (ACCA F4 GLO June 2011 model answer). [CORRECTED #11: chapter says "issued to a seller (or agent)"; the recipient is the shipper, who is often but not always the seller]
  • Convention wording: a document which evidences a contract of carriage by sea and the taking over or loading of the goods by the carrier, and by which the carrier undertakes to deliver the goods against surrender of the document (Hamburg Rules 1978, Art 1) [ADDED].
  • Trap: an FOB buyer who books the ship is the shipper; the bill is issued to it, not to the seller.

2.1.2 The three functions [M]

FunctionRuleAuthority
Receipt for the goodsRecords the goods received, including the leading marks for identification furnished by the shipper before loadingHague-Visby Rules Art III r.3 [ADDED]; chapter p.60
Evidence of the contract of carriageStates the terms on which the goods are carriedACCA June 2011 answer [ADDED]
Document of titleA negotiable ("to order") bill carries the right to the goods and to re-route the shipment; transferred by endorsement and delivery; the carrier delivers only against surrender of an originalACCA June 2011 answer; Hamburg Rules Art 1 [ADDED]; chapter p.60

Mnemonic RED — Receipt, Evidence of contract, Document of title. The bill evidences the carriage contract; it is not itself the contract, which is made at booking.

2.1.3 Operation [M]

  1. Shipper hands goods to the carrier; carrier issues the bill (chapter p.60).
  2. Bill "to order": the carrier holds the goods for the lawful holder; the shipper is the first holder (chapter: "the carrier therefore holds the goods on behalf of the seller").
  3. Seller presents the bill with the other documents to a bank to obtain payment (chapter), under a documentary credit or a collection against a bill of exchange.
  4. Seller endorses and delivers the bill; each transferee becomes holder in turn (chapter: "indorse the bill by signing it (negotiate it), thereby transferring title to the goods").
  5. The holder presents an original at destination; the carrier delivers against surrender (Hamburg Rules Art 1).
  • Under a credit: the bill must show the goods shipped on board a named vessel at the port of loading stated in the credit, by pre-printed wording or a dated on-board notation (UCP 600 Art 20(a)(ii)) and must be clean (Art 27, 2.1.4).
  • Trap: a photocopy does not entitle anyone to the goods; an original must be surrendered.
  • Applied (illustrative): Bank Z pays the seller under a credit and holds the endorsed bill; the buyer has not reimbursed it. The carrier may not release the cargo without an original; Bank Z controls delivery.

2.1.4 Types [M]

  • Tested as: "Which is a document of title?" (the negotiable bill of lading). "Which TWO are non-negotiable?" (straight bill; air waybill).
  • Applied (illustrative): a document "consigned to Bata Ltd", goods loaded on MV Kite, no adverse notations: a straight, shipped, clean bill of lading; Bata Ltd collects as named consignee.

2.1.5 Passing of risk [M — cross-reference]

Chapter p.60: the bill "evidences the timing of the passing of goods to the carrier; normally, therefore, also the passing of risk to the buyer ... unless the parties have separately agreed". The bill evidences the event; the sale contract (CISG Art 67 or the Incoterm) passes the risk. Those rules are B2(d) and B1(c).

2.2 Bank transfers (C1(b)) [level 1]

Not in the chapter. Describe the chain and label every participant with the Model Law term. Dec 2013: "the majority seemed to think that the sender had to be a bank"; "very few identified the point that originators were also senders".

2.2.1 The chain and its vocabulary [H]

Originator → payment order 1 → Originator's bank → payment order 2 → Intermediary bank (a correspondent, where the end banks have no account with each other) → payment order 3 → Beneficiary's bank → accepts for the beneficiary → Beneficiary.

Term (Model Law Art 2)DefinitionNote

Payment orderAn unconditional instruction, in any form, by a sender to a receiving bank to place at the disposal of a beneficiary a fixed or determinable amount of money"Pay when the goods arrive" is not a payment order
OriginatorThe issuer of the first payment order in a credit transferAlso a sender
BeneficiaryThe person designated in the originator's payment order to receive funds as a result of the credit transferNot "payee"

  • Remember: obligations run link by link between each sender and its receiving bank, not from the originator to a bank it never dealt with.
  • Trap: credit transfer is not a letter of credit: an unconditional movement of funds v a bank's conditional undertaking to pay against documents (Dec 2013: "a fairly common error").
  • Applied (illustrative): Vela BV (Rotterdam) instructs its Dutch bank to pay US$1m to Nusantara PT at Bank X (Jakarta) via a New York correspondent. Vela = originator and first sender; Dutch bank = receiving bank of order 1, sender of order 2, originator's bank; New York bank = intermediary bank; Bank X = beneficiary's bank; Nusantara = beneficiary.

Mnemonic O-S-R-I-B — Originator, Sender(s), Receiving bank(s), Intermediary bank, Beneficiary's bank, then the Beneficiary.

2.3 UNCITRAL Model Law on International Credit Transfers (C1(c)) [level 2]

Not in the chapter. Tested 2024–25 Q1, Dec 2016, Dec 2013. All content [ADDED: Model Law]. Arts 7, 9, 18 and 19 were confirmed from an official-body reproduction of the text (Appendix A notes this).

2.3.1 Nature and structure [M]

  • A model law is a template that binds no one until a State enacts it; a convention binds the States that ratify it. Adopted by UNCITRAL 15 May 1992 (uncitral.un.org). Enactment record: SOURCE DOES NOT ESTABLISH THIS.
  • Ch I Arts 1–4 general provisions; Ch II Arts 5–12 obligations of the parties; Ch III Arts 13–18 consequences of failed, erroneous or delayed transfers; Ch IV Art 19 completion.
  • Trap: "the Model Law has been ratified by X States" is a category error.

2.3.2 Obligations of the sender: Art 5 [H]

RuleContent
Own order, Art 5(1)A sender is bound by a payment order it issued or authorised
Authenticated order, Art 5(2)Where the order is subject to authentication other than mere comparison of signature, a purported sender who did not issue it is nevertheless bound if (a) the authentication was, in the circumstances, a commercially reasonable method of security against unauthorised payment orders and (b) the receiving bank complied with the authentication
No contracting out, Art 5(3)The parties may not agree that a purported sender is bound where the authentication is not commercially reasonable
Escape, Art 5(4)Not bound if the purported sender proves the order resulted from the actions of a person other than (a) a present or former employee or (b) a person whose relationship with it enabled access to the authentication procedure; but the escape fails if the bank proves that person gained access through the purported sender's fault
Payment, Art 5(6)A sender becomes obligated to pay the receiving bank for the payment order when the receiving bank accepts it, but payment is not due until the beginning of the execution period (2024–25 Q1)

Decision tree: who bears an unauthorised order

  1. Issued or authorised by the sender? Yes → bound. No → 2.
  2. Authentication beyond signature comparison? No → not bound. Yes → 3.
  3. Commercially reasonable and complied with? No → not bound (no agreement can say otherwise). Yes → 4.
  4. Sender proves the order came from an outsider (not employee, former employee or access-holder)? No → bound. Yes → 5.
  5. Bank proves the outsider got access through the sender's fault? Yes → bound. No → not bound.
  • Remember: Art 5(6) in one line — arises on acceptance, owed to the receiving bank, due at the start of the execution period. Both halves say "receiving bank"; the 2024–25 distractors swapped in "sending bank".
  • Applied (illustrative): a dismissed bookkeeper uses Kestrel Ltd's online-banking token to send a US$400,000 order; the bank verifies it under the agreed token procedure. Kestrel is bound: commercially reasonable procedure, complied with, former employee. An outside hacker who breached the bank's own systems, with no fault of Kestrel: not bound.

2.3.3 Acceptance and rejection: Arts 7 and 9 [H]

  • A receiving bank accepts on receipt where it has agreed with the sender to execute that sender's orders on receipt (Arts 7(2)(a), 9(1)(a)).
  • A receiving bank that gives no notice of rejection within the time allowed is deemed to have accepted (Arts 7(2)(e), 9(1)(h)). Where the sender has sufficient funds, notice of rejection must be given no later than the banking day following the end of the execution period (Arts 7(3), 9(2)).
  • Consequence: acceptance triggers the sender's payment obligation (Art 5(6)); acceptance by the beneficiary's bank completes the transfer (Art 19).
  • Trap: "a bank is never bound until it acts" is false: silence binds.
  • Applied (illustrative): Bank Y receives an order on Tuesday, the sender's account has sufficient funds, and Bank Y does nothing. By the end of Wednesday no rejection has been sent: deemed acceptance, and the sender's payment obligation arose then.

2.3.4 Execution and revocation [H]

  • An accepting bank must carry out the order within the execution period; a bank that fails to do so is liable for interest (Art 17, verified; the duty it presupposes sits in Arts 8 and 11, whose wording is in Appendix A).
  • A revocation order must be authenticated (Art 12). The timing rule is in Appendix A pending verification.
  • Trap: execution (the bank's own onward order) is not completion (Art 19).

2.3.5 Remedies: Arts 17 and 18 [H]

  • A receiving bank that does not execute in time is liable for interest on the amount for the period of delay; its liability is for interest only, not consequential loss (Art 17).
  • The remedies in Art 17 are exclusive; no other remedy under other doctrines of law is available for non-compliance with Arts 8 or 10, except where a bank improperly executed, or failed to execute, a payment order (a) with the specific intent to cause loss, or (b) recklessly and with actual knowledge that loss would be likely to result (Art 18).
  • The refund rule for an uncompleted transfer (Art 14, the "money-back guarantee") is in Appendix A: heading confirmed, text pending.
  • Trap: negligence is not the exception; intent or recklessness is. Lost profits are not recoverable.
  • Applied (illustrative): a bank executes two days late; Nusantara PT loses a contract. Nusantara's claim is limited to interest for two days; the lost contract is not recoverable from any bank unless intent or recklessness with knowledge is proved.

2.3.6 Completion: Art 19 [H]

  • A credit transfer is completed when the beneficiary's bank accepts a payment order for the benefit of the beneficiary; on completion the beneficiary's bank becomes indebted to the beneficiary to the extent of the order accepted (Art 19(1)).
  • Completion occurs notwithstanding that a receiving bank has deducted charges from the amount transferred (Art 19(2)). "The beneficiary received less" does not mean the transfer failed.
  • Applied (illustrative): Bank X accepts the order for Nusantara PT on Thursday and posts the credit on Friday. The transfer completed on Thursday; from then Bank X, not Vela or the Dutch bank, owes Nusantara.

Mnemonic A-P-E-C — Acceptance (Arts 7, 9) → Payment obligation (Art 5(6)) → Execution (or interest, Art 17) → Completion (Art 19).

2.4 UN Convention on International Bills of Exchange and International Promissory Notes (C1(d)) [level 2]

Chapter pp.57–59. Tested 2025–26 Q1 (acceptance), Q2 (definite sum), Dec 2017 (transfer by delivery). "A highly complex area of the syllabus."

2.4.1 Status [M]

  • Adopted by the UN General Assembly on 9 December 1988 (Res 43/165); opt-in; not in force: it enters into force on the first day of the month following twelve months after deposit of the tenth instrument of ratification, acceptance, approval or accession (Art 89(1)); five States party at the date of the UNCITRAL status source [ADDED: uncitral.un.org].
  • It does not apply to cheques (Art 1).
  • Trap: ratified (convention) v enacted (model law).

2.4.2 Definitions and parties [H]

Bill of exchange (Art 3(1)) [CORRECTED #1]: a written instrument which (a) contains an unconditional order whereby the drawer directs the drawee to pay a definite sum of money to the payee or to his order; (b) is payable on demand or at a definite time; (c) is dated; (d) is signed by the drawer.

Promissory note (Art 3(2)) [ADDED]: a written instrument which (a) contains an unconditional promise whereby the maker undertakes to pay a definite sum of money to the payee or to his order; (b) is payable on demand or at a definite time; (c) is dated; (d) is signed by the maker.

The chapter's definition (p.57) is the UK Bills of Exchange Act 1882 s.3(1): "sum certain", "fixed or determinable future time", "or to bearer", no date. Under the Convention there is no bearer alternative and the instrument must be dated.

2.4.3 When the Convention applies: Arts 1 and 2 [H]

  • Art 1: the instrument must contain the heading and, in its text, the words "International bill of exchange (UNCITRAL Convention)" or "International promissory note (UNCITRAL Convention)" [CORRECTED #2].
  • Art 2: it must specify at least two of five places and indicate that any two so specified are in different States: where drawn; next to the drawer's signature; next to the drawee's name; next to the payee's name; the place of payment [CORRECTED #3: the chapter's "where the drawer signed" is the place stated beside the signature].
  • Mnemonic D-D-D-P-P — Drawn, Drawer's signature, Drawee's name, Payee's name, Place of payment.
  • Trap: places written on the instrument, not the parties' nationality or residence; words without international places, or places without the words, both fail.
  • Applied (illustrative): "Drawn at Lagos" beside the drawer's signature and "Payable at Bank K, Rotterdam": international. All stated places in Nigeria: not, whatever the drawee's nationality.

2.4.4 A definite sum: Art 7 and the interest rule [H]

The sum payable is deemed to be a definite sum although the instrument states that it is to be paid: (a) with interest; (b) by instalments at successive dates; (c) by instalments at successive dates with a stipulation that upon default in payment of any instalment the unpaid balance becomes due; (d) according to a rate of exchange indicated in the instrument or to be determined as directed by the instrument; or (e) in a currency other than the currency in which the sum is expressed (Art 7) [ADDED].

  • A stipulation for interest is deemed not written unless it indicates the rate; the rate may vary by reference to published reference rates within stipulated limits, and may not be subject to unilateral determination by a person named in the instrument [ADDED: UNCITRAL explanatory note].
  • Mnemonic I-I-A-X-C — Interest, Instalments, Acceleration, eXchange rate, Currency.
  • Trap: 2025–26 Q2 distractors: "only if the interest is at a fixed rate" (false); "single payments rather than instalments" (false). Correct: exchange-rate and other-currency bills.
  • Applied (illustrative): "EUR 100,000 payable in USD at the ECB rate on the due date, interest at EURIBOR plus 2%": a definite sum. "With interest" and no rate: the interest words are treated as not written; the bill stands for EUR 100,000.

2.4.5 Time of payment: Art 9 [M]

  • Payable at a definite time if payable on a stated date, at a fixed period after a stated date or after the date of the instrument, at a fixed period after sight, or by instalments (with or without acceleration) (Arts 7, 9) [CORRECTED #20: not "fixed or determinable future time"].
  • For a bill payable at a fixed period after sight, time runs from the date of acceptance or, if dishonoured by non-acceptance, from the date of protest or, if protest is dispensed with, from the date of dishonour (Art 9).
  • Applied (illustrative): "90 days after sight", accepted 1 October 2026: matures 30 December 2026.

2.4.6 Transfer, endorsement and the holder [H]

  • An instrument is transferred by endorsement and delivery or, if the last endorsement is in blank, by mere delivery (Dec 2017) [CORRECTED #4: the chapter's "bearer bill"].
  • An endorsement must be unconditional [ADDED: UNCITRAL explanatory note]. A blank endorsement is a signature alone, or a signature with a statement that the instrument is payable to a person in possession of it; a special endorsement names the endorsee (chapter: "in favour of a specified person").
  • Holder (Art 15): (a) the payee in possession; or (b) a person in possession of an instrument endorsed to him, or on which the last endorsement is in blank, and on which there appears an uninterrupted series of endorsements, even if any endorsement was forged or signed by an agent without authority.
  • Trap: possession alone is not enough where the last special endorsement names someone else.
  • Applied (illustrative): Orion GmbH signs the back without naming Bank P: blank endorsement. Bank P is a holder and may pass the bill to Bank Q by delivery; Bank Q is a holder too; neither signed, so neither is liable on the bill (2.4.8).

2.4.7 Protected holder [H]

  • Protected holder (Art 29): the holder of an instrument which was complete when he took it, or which was incomplete and was completed in accordance with authority given, provided that when he became holder he was without knowledge of the defences and claims specified [ADDED].
  • Every holder is presumed to be a protected holder unless the contrary is proved [ADDED].
  • The consequence (the defences a party may and may not raise against a protected holder) is in Appendix A pending confirmation of Art 30's wording.
  • Trap: "for value" is an 1882 Act requirement, not a Convention condition.

2.4.8 Liability of the parties [H]

  • Acceptance (2025–26 Q1): written on the bill; the drawee's signature with "accepted" or words of similar import, or the drawee's signature alone. Not: "accepted" alone; the date alone. Chapter p.58: by signing, the drawee becomes the acceptor and accepts liability.
  • Drawer: liable if the bill is dishonoured; may exclude or limit liability by an express stipulation on the bill ("without recourse"), effective only if another party is or becomes liable (Art 38) [ADDED].
  • Maker: liable according to the note; a stipulation excluding or limiting the maker's liability is ineffective (Art 39) [ADDED].
  • Endorsers and guarantors: chapter p.58: all prior parties who signed the bill before it reached the holder are liable on dishonour, subject to protest and notice (2.4.9).
  • Transferor by mere delivery: not a signatory, so not liable on the instrument; represents to the transferee only that the instrument bears no forged or unauthorised signature, has not been materially altered, and that the transferor knows of no fact impairing the transferee's right to payment (Art 45(1)) [CORRECTED #5].
  • Guarantee (aval): given by "guaranteed", "aval", "good as aval" or words of similar import with the guarantor's signature, or by a signature alone on the front of the instrument; if no party is specified, the guarantee is for the acceptor or drawee (bill) or the maker (note) [ADDED: UNCITRAL explanatory note] [CORRECTED #7].
  • Applied (illustrative): the drawee writes "accepted" but does not sign: no acceptance; the holder's recourse is against the drawer and endorsers. Bank Q passes a blank-endorsed bill to Bank R without signing and the acceptor dishonours: Bank R sues the acceptor, drawer and endorsers, not Bank Q.

2.4.9 Dishonour, protest, notice and limitation [H]

  • Chapter p.58: if the acceptor refuses to pay on presentment, the bill is dishonoured.
  • Protest for dishonour by non-acceptance or non-payment must be made on the day of dishonour or on one of the four business days which follow [ADDED].
  • Notice of dishonour must be given within the two business days which follow; each endorser who receives notice must notify its own endorser within two business days [ADDED].
  • A right of action arising on an instrument may no longer be exercised after four years have elapsed (Art 84) [ADDED].
  • Mnemonic 4-2-4 — protest four business days; notice two business days; limitation four years.
  • Applied (illustrative): dishonoured on Monday 2 March 2027: protest by Friday 6 March; notice by Wednesday 4 March; action possible until 2031.

2.4.10 Promissory notes [H]

  • A promise, not an order: maker and payee; no drawee; no acceptance (Art 3(2)).
  • The maker is primarily liable from signature and cannot exclude that liability (Art 39). Contrast the drawer's "without recourse".
  • Words: "International promissory note (UNCITRAL Convention)" in heading and text (Art 1); internationality by Art 2; unspecified guarantee is for the maker.
  • Applied (illustrative): Solano SA signs "We promise to pay Rialto Ltd or order USD 200,000 on 30 June 2027", headed with the Convention words, dated, made in Buenos Aires, payable in London: Solano is maker and primarily liable; a "maker not liable" clause is ineffective.

2.4.11 Life of a Convention bill [H]

  1. Drawer draws: Convention words, two places in different States, definite sum, date, signature (Arts 1–3).
  2. Drawee signs → acceptor, primarily liable; refusal → dishonour → protest and notice → recourse against the drawer.
  3. Payee holds to maturity or transfers by endorsement and delivery, typically discounting to a bank (chapter p.58).
  4. Special or blank endorsements continue the chain; after a blank endorsement, delivery alone transfers.
  5. Paid → discharge. Dishonoured → protest within four business days, notice within two → recourse against acceptor, drawer, endorsers, guarantors (not a transferor by delivery) within four years.

2.5 Letters of credit and letters of comfort (C1(e)) [level 2]

Chapter pp.62–63. Not tested in the four current reports. The chapter never names UCP 600, treats credits as revocable or irrevocable and has the advising bank paying; corrected below.

2.5.1 What a documentary credit is [H]

  • Means (chapter p.62): a means whereby the seller obtains payment against documents from a bank while the buyer enjoys a period of credit before paying.
  • "Credit means any arrangement, however named or described, that is irrevocable and thereby constitutes a definite undertaking of the issuing bank to honour a complying presentation" (UCP 600 Art 2) [ADDED].
  • UCP 600: ICC Uniform Customs and Practice for Documentary Credits, 2007 revision, ICC Publication No. 600, approved 25 October 2006, in effect 1 July 2007 [ADDED: iccwbo.org].
  • "A credit by its nature is a separate transaction from the sale or other contract on which it may be based. Banks are in no way concerned with or bound by such contract, even if any reference whatsoever to it is included in the credit." (Art 4). The chapter's "letters of credit should be arranged before the contract is entered into" is retained as practice advice [CORRECTED #19].
  • Trap: credit transfer ≠ letter of credit; a letter of credit is not a negotiable instrument.

2.5.3 The mechanism: the chapter's eight steps corrected [H]

  1. Sale contract stipulates payment by credit; B asks BB to issue a credit in favour of S (step 1).
  2. BB issues the credit → irrevocably bound to honour a complying presentation (Arts 2, 7) (step 2).
  3. BB asks SB to advise S; SB advises without undertaking (Art 9(a)) (step 3).
  4. If authorised or requested and it agrees, SB confirms → second undertaking (Arts 2, 8) (step 4).
  5. S ships and presents the stipulated documents (transport document, invoice, insurance and any others required) [CORRECTED #14: not "proof of delivery of the goods"; banks deal with documents, Art 5] (step 5).
  6. SB examines within a maximum of five banking days following the day of presentation (Art 14(b)); a confirming bank honours or negotiates and forwards the documents; an advising bank forwards them without paying [CORRECTED #15] (step 6).
  7. BB examines within five banking days and, if complying, honours and reimburses SB (Art 7) (step 7).
  8. BB debits B's account and releases the documents; B uses the bill of lading to collect the goods (step 8).

A credit must state whether it is available by sight payment, deferred payment, acceptance or negotiation (Art 6(b)): in plain terms, paid at once; paid at a later fixed date; the bank accepts a bill of exchange drawn on it and pays at maturity; a bank purchases the documents and advances funds.

2.5.5 Letters of comfort [H]

  • Means (chapter p.63): a letter, typically from a parent or holding company to a lender to its subsidiary, indicating an intention to continue to support the subsidiary; used where the subsidiary is raising finance; "normally it is just a comfort"; "rarely does such a letter bind the parent", so on the subsidiary's insolvency the parent is not liable for its obligations.
  • Authority: Kleinwort Benson Ltd v Malaysia Mining Corporation Bhd [1989] 1 WLR 379 (CA): the comfort letter had no contractual effect; the court ascertains the parties' common intention from the language of the document and the surrounding circumstances (Ralph Gibson LJ); a document called a "letter of comfort" can nevertheless create contractual obligations if so construed [ADDED: BAILII-hosted judgments citing the case]. The facts and the "present policy v future promise" formulation are in Appendix A pending a law report.
  • Contrast [ADDED: general knowledge]: comfort letter (usually no obligation) v guarantee (secondary promise to pay if the debtor defaults) v indemnity (primary promise to make good the loss).
  • Trap: the label does not decide; the words do. A lender who accepts comfort instead of a guarantee has priced that risk.
  • Applied (illustrative): Kanto Holdings writes "we are aware of the facility and intend to maintain our shareholding in the borrower". The subsidiary defaults. Nothing in the letter is a promise to pay; the lender has no claim. "We undertake to fund the subsidiary to repay the facility" would be construed as a promise.

5. MCQ Traps & Discrimination Lines

  1. A bill of lading is issued by the carrier to the shipper; "seller" is wrong where the buyer booked the carriage. [M]
  2. Functions: receipt, evidence of the carriage contract, document of title; only the negotiable "to order" bill is a document of title. [H]
  3. An "airway bill" is an air waybill: non-negotiable and not a bill of lading. [M]
  4. The bill evidences shipment; the sale contract passes risk. [M]
  5. Clean = no notation of defective condition (Art 27); credits need on-board evidence (Art 20(a)(ii)). [M]
  6. Delivery only against an original; a copy entitles no one. [M]
  7. Sender = originator and every sending bank; need not be a bank (Dec 2013). [H]
  8. Sender pays the receiving bank on the receiving bank's acceptance; due at the start of the execution period; both halves say receiving bank (2024–25 Q1). [H]
  9. Credit transfer ≠ letter of credit. [H]
  10. Payment order must be unconditional. [H]
  11. Silence past the rejection deadline is acceptance. [H]
  12. Authentication: bound if commercially reasonable and complied with; escape only for an outsider without the sender's fault; no contracting out downward. [H]
  13. Revocation must be authenticated. [M]
  14. Delay → interest only; exception intent or recklessness with knowledge, not negligence. [H]
  15. Completion = beneficiary's bank accepts; deducted charges do not prevent it. [H]
  16. Model law enacted; convention ratified. [M]
  17. Convention bill: payee or order, definite sum, dated, signed; "bearer" and "sum certain" signal the 1882 Act. [H]
  18. Words in heading and text, including "(UNCITRAL Convention)". [H]
  19. Two of five places in different States; not nationality. [H]
  20. Cheques excluded. [M]
  21. Ten ratifications, then twelve months; not in force. [M]
  22. Definite sum survives interest, instalments, acceleration, exchange rate, other currency; "only if fixed" and "single payments" are false (2025–26 Q2). [H]
  23. "With interest", no rate: interest words disregarded, bill valid. [H]
  24. Acceptance: signature alone or with "accepted"; not the word alone or a date (2025–26 Q1). [H]
  25. Drawee not liable until acceptance; acceptor primary; maker primary from signature. [H]
  26. Drawer may exclude ("without recourse", Art 38); maker may not (Art 39). [H]
  27. Blank endorsement → transfer by delivery (Dec 2017); not a "bearer bill". [H]
  28. A transferor by mere delivery is not liable on the bill; Art 45(1) representations only. [H]
  29. Every holder presumed protected; no "for value". [H]
  30. Protest four business days; notice two business days; limitation four years. [H]
  31. Guarantee: "guaranteed"/"aval"/"good as aval" or signature alone on the front; not "in case of need". [M]
  32. Note: promise; maker; no acceptance. [H]
  33. UCP 600 credits are irrevocable even if silent; "revocable" is a distractor. [H]
  34. Amendment needs issuing bank, confirming bank (if any) and beneficiary; not notification; not the applicant. [H]
  35. Issuing must honour; confirming must honour or negotiate; advising has no undertaking. [H]
  36. "Undertaking", not "guarantee". [H]
  37. Autonomy (Art 4) and documents not goods (Art 5): a goods dispute is no defence to payment. [H]
  38. Five banking days to examine. [H]
  39. Confirmed = two undertakings; unconfirmed = one. [H]
  40. Comfort letter: normally moral, not legal; the words decide (Kleinwort Benson). [H]
  41. "Which TWO": both needed; find the shared feature. NOT is capitalised. Similar words: underline who pays whom. Added restrictions ("only", "must be fixed", "must be a bank") usually make a true rule false. [H]
